# Chapter 9 — EQ: Carving the Frequency Spectrum

Equalization (**EQ**) is the most-used tool in mixing, and it follows directly
from Chapter 1: every sound is a blend of frequencies, and EQ lets you turn
bands of those frequencies **up or down**. That's it. With it you remove
problems (mud, harshness, rumble), help instruments fit together, and shape
character. This chapter covers how EQ works and gives concrete starting points
for **guitar, bass, keyboard and vocals** — but treat every number as a place to
start, not a rule.

## 9.1 What EQ is (and what it isn't)

An EQ is a set of **filters** that boost or cut the level of chosen frequency
ranges. It is a **linear** process (Chapter 5): it changes the *balance* of
frequencies that already exist — it never adds new harmonics. (When a plugin
*does* add harmonics, that's saturation/distortion, Chapter 10, not EQ.)

The mental model: picture the frequency spectrum (20 Hz on the left → 20 kHz on
the right, spaced logarithmically per Chapter 1). EQ lets you push any region of
that line up (boost) or down (cut).

## 9.2 The filter types

Every EQ is built from a handful of filter shapes:

- **High-pass filter (HPF) / low-cut.** Removes everything *below* a chosen
  frequency. The single most useful move in mixing — cleans out rumble and
  sub-mud. (Confusingly named: it *passes the highs*, i.e. cuts the lows.)
- **Low-pass filter (LPF) / high-cut.** Removes everything *above* a chosen
  frequency. Tames fizz, harshness, hiss.
- **Bell / peak.** Boosts or cuts a band centered on a frequency. The workhorse.
  Its width is set by **Q** (see below).
- **Shelf (high or low).** Boosts or cuts everything above (high shelf) or below
  (low shelf) a frequency by a fixed amount — like a gentle tilt. Great for broad
  "more air" or "more warmth" moves.
- **Notch.** A very narrow, deep cut to surgically kill a single problem
  frequency (a resonant ring, hum, feedback).

### Q — bandwidth

**Q** sets how wide or narrow a bell filter is. **Low Q = wide** (affects a broad
range, sounds natural and musical — good for tone shaping). **High Q = narrow**
(affects a pinpoint range — good for surgically removing a specific problem).
Rule of thumb: **boost wide, cut narrow.** Broad boosts sound musical; narrow
cuts remove problems without thinning everything.

## 9.3 Subtractive vs. additive EQ

Two philosophies, both valid, but lead with the first:

- **Subtractive EQ (cut first).** Remove what's *wrong* — mud, boxiness,
  harshness, rumble. Cutting is less likely to cause harshness or clipping than
  boosting, and it cleans up a mix without adding energy. **Default to cutting.**
- **Additive EQ (boost to enhance).** Add what's *missing* — a touch of "air,"
  some presence, a little warmth. Use sparingly and broadly.

### The "sweep and destroy" technique

To *find* a problem frequency: take a bell, set a **narrow, high-Q boost**, sweep
it across the spectrum until the nasty resonance jumps out and sounds worst, then
**flip that boost into a cut** and widen it slightly. This finds mud, boxiness,
and harshness fast.

## 9.4 EQ in context: it's about the whole mix

The most important principle: **EQ for the mix, not for the soloed track.** An
instrument that sounds amazing alone often clashes when everything plays
together, because instruments **share frequency ranges** and fight ("masking" —
Chapter 3's psychoacoustics in action). The art is **carving complementary
spaces**: cut a little where one instrument should sit so another can own that
range. Classic example: cut the bass guitar slightly where the kick drum lives,
or carve a dip in the guitars where the lead vocal sits.

## 9.5 The frequency map (memorize these zones)

The whole spectrum, in plain language:

| Zone | Range | Character | Common move |
|------|-------|-----------|-------------|
| **Sub-bass** | 20–60 Hz | Felt more than heard; rumble | HPF most non-bass tracks here |
| **Bass** | 60–250 Hz | Fundamental weight, power | Where mud also lives |
| **Low-mids** | 250–500 Hz | Warmth — or **"mud"/boxiness"** | Most common cut zone |
| **Mids** | 500 Hz–2 kHz | Body, "honk," attack of most instruments | Cut "boxy" ~400–600 Hz; "nasal" ~1 kHz |
| **High-mids** | 2–6 kHz | Presence, clarity, attack, "bite" | Boost for cut-through; harshness lives here too |
| **Highs** | 6–12 kHz | Brilliance, sibilance, detail | Sibilance ~5–9 kHz |
| **Air** | 12–20 kHz | Openness, "expensive" sheen | Gentle high shelf for sparkle |

Anchor to remember: **mud lives in the low-mids (~200–500 Hz); presence and
clarity live in the high-mids (~2–6 kHz); harshness also lives in the high-mids,
so the same region gives both magic and pain.**

## 9.6 Starting points by instrument

> Reminder: these are **starting points**, not rules. Sweep around every value
> and judge in the full mix. See **Appendix A** for a consolidated chart.

### Electric guitar

- **High-pass ~80–100 Hz.** Electric guitar has little useful content below
  this; cutting it clears room for bass and kick.
- **The power is in 200 Hz–1 kHz.** This midrange is where the "meat" and body
  of guitars live — don't gut it chasing scooped tones in a dense mix.
- **Mud / boxiness: cut around 300–500 Hz** if it sounds congested.
- **Presence / bite: boost gently around 2.5–5 kHz** to cut through.
- **Fizz: low-pass or shelf down above ~6–8 kHz** if the distortion is harsh
  (often a cab/IR issue — Chapter 6).
- For **multiple guitars**, carve them differently (one slightly brighter, one
  warmer) so they don't mask each other.

### Acoustic guitar

- **High-pass ~70–100 Hz** — mics pick up low rumble/handling that muddies the
  mix; clean it out.
- **Boom / mud: cut around 150–300 Hz** if it's boomy (often from a tight
  recording space or proximity).
- **Boxiness: cut around 500 Hz–1 kHz** if it sounds "honky."
- **Sparkle / string detail: gentle boost ~8–12 kHz** for shimmer.
- **Pick attack / clarity: ~2–5 kHz** if it needs to cut through.

### Bass (guitar)

- **Fundamental and weight: 60–250 Hz.** The "magic" of bass lives roughly
  **80–200 Hz** — the fundamentals and the punch.
- **Sub / bottom: 40–80 Hz** for the deep extension (mind your speakers/room
  when judging this).
- **Boom: cut around 180–250 Hz** if it's too "boomy," while preserving the
  fundamentals below.
- **Definition / "growl" / finger or pick attack: 700 Hz–2.5 kHz.** This is what
  lets bass be *heard* on small speakers (phones, laptops) that can't reproduce
  the lows.
- **String/pick clack: ~2.5–5 kHz** if you want articulation to poke through.
- Sit it with the **kick drum**: decide who owns the deep lows and carve the
  other slightly there so they don't fight.

### Keyboard / piano / synth

- **Most attention in 50–250 Hz** — treat boom and mud here to keep it lush and
  warm without clouding the mix.
- **High-pass** anything that doesn't need deep lows (e.g., a bright synth pad,
  organ) so it doesn't crowd bass and kick.
- **Mud / boxiness: cut around 200–500 Hz** if it's congesting the low-mids.
- **Presence / clarity: gentle boost ~3–6 kHz** to bring a piano or lead synth
  forward.
- **Air / sparkle: high shelf ~10–14 kHz** for a polished, open top end.
- Pianos and pads are wide-band and easily mask vocals/guitars — **carve a dip
  where the lead element sits** rather than turning the whole thing down.

### Vocals

- **Always high-pass — minimum ~80–100 Hz** (higher for thin/female voices,
  lower for deep voices). Removes rumble, plosive thumps and proximity build-up.
- **Mud / "chestiness": cut around 200–400 Hz** if it sounds thick or boxy.
- **Boxiness / honk: cut around 500 Hz–1 kHz** if needed.
- **Presence / intelligibility: boost ~3–6 kHz** so consonants and lyrics cut
  through (for many male voices, clarity sits ~4–9 kHz).
- **Warmth/body** for a voice often lives ~85–250 Hz (e.g. ~100–150 Hz for
  fullness) — boost gently if thin, but watch for mud.
- **Air / "expensive" sheen: high shelf ~10–16 kHz.**
- **Sibilance ("sss"/"shh") lives ~5–9 kHz** — but don't fix it with EQ (you'd
  dull the whole voice); use a **de-esser** (Chapter 10) which only ducks the
  harsh frequency *when* the "s" happens.

## 9.7 Common EQ mistakes

- **Soloing too much.** EQ in context (9.4).
- **Boosting everything.** If you boost every track, you've changed nothing
  relative — and you've eaten your headroom. **Cut more than you boost.**
- **Over-narrow boosts.** Narrow boosts sound unnatural and ringy. Boost wide.
- **Ignoring the low end.** Un-HPF'd tracks pile up sub-mud you can't hear on
  small speakers but that wastes headroom. HPF generously.
- **EQ'ing the symptom, not the source.** Harsh fizzy guitar? Try a different
  IR/mic position (Chapter 6) before reaching for a steep cut.

## Summary

- EQ boosts/cuts **bands of frequencies** using filters: **HPF/LPF, bell, shelf,
  notch**; **Q** sets bandwidth (**boost wide, cut narrow**).
- **Cut first (subtractive)** to remove mud/harshness; **boost sparingly** to
  enhance. Use **sweep-and-destroy** to find problems.
- **EQ in the full mix**, carving complementary spaces so instruments don't mask
  each other.
- Know the map: **mud ~200–500 Hz, presence/clarity ~2–6 kHz, air >10 kHz.**
- **High-pass almost everything** that isn't bass/kick (vocals/guitars ~80–100
  Hz). Per-instrument starting points above and in **Appendix A**.

---

> **Try this.** On any track, insert an EQ, make a narrow +6 dB bell, and sweep
> it slowly from lows to highs. *Listen* to how each region sounds — find the
> "mud" around 300 Hz, the "honk" around 1 kHz, the "harsh" around 3 kHz, the
> "air" up top. This single exercise builds the frequency intuition that every
> mixing decision relies on.
